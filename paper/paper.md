# Architects or Accelerants? Social Media, Artificial Intelligence, and the Radicalization Process

Sep 17, 2026 · @Joe

## Introduction

In October 2018, a socially isolated man who had posted 627 times on the low-moderation platform Gab in ten months walked into the Tree of Life Synagogue in Pittsburgh and killed eleven worshippers, the deadliest antisemitic attack in American history (Amman, Kupper, and Meloy 2026). In February 2020, a gunman in Hanau, Germany killed nine people at a shisha bar and a kiosk, leaving a twenty-four-page manifesto braiding racist conspiracy theory with pages of grievance against women (Jasser et al. 2020). In December 2021, a young man was arrested on the grounds of Windsor Castle carrying a crossbow, having discussed his plan to kill the Queen with an artificial intelligence companion he built on the Replika platform and named Sarai, exchanging more than five thousand messages with it (Glazzard, McIlhatton, and Martin 2026; Kunst et al. 2026). In July 2024, a seventeen-year-old in Southport, England murdered three girls aged six to nine at a dance class (Ware 2026). In May 2025, a sixteen-year-old in Pirkkala, Finland stabbed three female classmates after six months of preparation, sending a newspaper a one-page manifesto he appears to have drafted with the help of ChatGPT (Solea 2025). And in June 2025, federal prosecutors in Manhattan charged a man who allegedly built improvised explosive devices from chemicals bought online and told investigators he had used artificial intelligence to learn which chemicals to purchase and mix (United States v. Gann 2025).

These six attacks and plots span four countries, several ideological profiles, and seven years. What they share is that each perpetrator's path to violence ran through a screen. That observation has become the organizing assumption of contemporary counterterrorism practice. Ware (2026) describes social media as now entrenched as the front line of the extremist threat, and the framework advanced by Kunst et al. (2026) goes further, arguing that recommender systems, generative models, and synthetic social validation have combined into what the authors call an AI architecture of radicalization.

That claim is worth examining rather than assuming, because a competing body of evidence says something close to the opposite. Shaw (2023), summarizing a large study of YouTube users, reports that algorithmic recommendation supplies only a very small share of the traffic reaching extremist videos, and that the people who consume such content overwhelmingly held resentful attitudes before they arrived. Metzler and Garcia (2024) conclude from a review of evidence on algorithmic media that algorithms mostly reinforce social drivers that already exist rather than creating them. Glazzard, McIlhatton, and Martin (2026) argue that fears about generative AI rest more on speculative worst-case scenarios than on demonstrated use, and that becoming a terrorist remains what it has always been: a socially embedded, contingent, human process in which technology plays an enabling rather than a causal role.

The field therefore says two things at once. One camp holds that the machine builds the path. The other holds that the machine paves a path human beings were already walking. The distinction is not academic. If the first account is right, the correct homeland security response is aggressive intervention in platform design, recommendation systems, and model behavior, because the technology is generating the threat. If the second is right, that response will consume enormous resources, impose real costs on speech and privacy, and leave the actual drivers of political violence untouched.

The stakes are concrete. Mehra and Herbach (2026) report that nearly thirty percent of individuals arrested on terrorism suspicion in European Union member states in 2024 were between twelve and twenty years old, and that most juvenile extremist offenders in their ninety-eight-case dataset were convicted of preparatory offenses or of possessing and distributing extremist material rather than of violent acts. The threat population is getting younger, its offenses are increasingly informational rather than kinetic, and Ware (2026) notes that this renders much of the kill-or-capture apparatus built after 2001 largely irrelevant. The regulatory environment is also shifting quickly. Meta agreed in August 2026 to pay up to $17.1 billion and to restrict engagement-driving features for teenagers, a settlement Kang and Tan (2026) describe as a potential inflection point for an industry that had largely escaped scrutiny. Decisions about how to govern algorithmic and generative systems are being made now, and they are being made on contested evidence.

This paper argues that the available evidence supports a middle position with sharp policy consequences. Artificial intelligence and social media function as accelerants and environments rather than as causes of radicalization, but they have helped produce a threat population the classical models handle badly: young, ideologically thin or ideologically mixed, fixated on violence without a program, and unattached to any organization. That shift, rather than algorithmic recruitment, is what should concern homeland security practitioners most, and the arrival of conversational AI extends it. The paper defines the issue and the current response, states a research question and method, reviews three competing scholarly perspectives, tests those perspectives against six cases, analyzes the evidence on which each perspective rests, and closes with findings and recommendations.

## Background: The Issue and the Current Response

### Defining radicalization

Radicalization has no settled definition, and that instability is itself part of the problem this paper examines. Hafez and Mullins (2015) survey the post-9/11 empirical literature and settle on a working consensus: radicalization is a gradual process of socialization into an extremist belief system that sets the stage for, but does not guarantee, violence. Their most useful contribution for present purposes is a distinction they draw from Borum and others between two things that are often collapsed. Cognitive radicalization involves acquiring values, attitudes, and beliefs that deviate sharply from those of mainstream society. Behavioral radicalization involves participating in radical activities that could culminate in terrorism. The combination usually precedes violence, but it does not make violence inevitable (Hafez and Mullins 2015, 961).

That gap between belief and action is the single most important fact in this literature, and it is why prediction has failed. Schmid observes that most radicals never turn to terrorism, that many terrorists were recruited first and radicalized afterward, and that some were never radicals at all; the result is a false positive rate that makes profiling nearly useless. Hafez and Mullins reject linear stage models altogether, proposing instead a puzzle metaphor in which four interlocking factors combine differently in every case: grievances, networks, ideology, and enabling environments and support structures (Hafez and Mullins 2015, 959). They explicitly caution against checklist-style indicators.

Simi and Windisch (2017) invert the question in a way that will matter later in this paper. Following Hirschi's logic in criminology, they ask not why people commit mass casualty terrorism but why the overwhelming majority of committed extremists do not. From life history interviews with former extremists they identify four barriers: sorting mechanisms away from mass casualty violence, changes in focus or availability, disillusionment, and moral apprehension. Most of their subjects encountered more than one.

### The theoretical mechanisms

Several bodies of theory explain why people move toward extreme groups in the first place, and any claim about technology has to connect to them. Hogg and Adelman (2013) advance uncertainty-identity theory: feelings of uncertainty about the self drive people toward groups that reduce it, and the groups best suited to that task are distinctive, clearly bounded, hierarchically organized, and ideologically homogeneous, which is a fair description of an extremist group. Whitehouse (2018) offers identity fusion, a visceral sense of oneness with a group in which personal and group identity become functionally equivalent, produced by intense shared experience or perceived shared biology, and associated with willingness to die for the group. Kristinsdottir, Ebner, and Whitehouse (2025) combine fusion with the clinical concept of extreme overvalued belief, arguing that fixation alone rarely produces violence and that fusion is the missing fourth ingredient alongside perceived outgroup threat, demonization, and endorsement of violence.

On the technology side, Suler (2004) supplies the oldest and still most cited mechanism: the online disinhibition effect, in which dissociative anonymity, invisibility, asynchronicity, solipsistic introjection, dissociative imagination, and minimization of authority combine to loosen ordinary restraint. Suler is careful to note the effect runs in both directions, toward unusual kindness as well as toward hatred and threat.

### The environment: from feed to model

The environment in which these mechanisms now operate has changed twice in twenty years. St. John (2006) captured the first change as it happened, reporting on the launch of the Facebook News Feed, which converted a site people visited into a system that pushed a continuously updating stream of other people's activity at them, and provoked immediate user backlash over the sense of being tracked. What followed was a decade of optimization. Internal Facebook documents reported by Merrill and Oremus (2021) show that from 2017 the ranking algorithm weighted emoji reactions five times more heavily than likes on the theory that provocative posts drive engagement; the company's own data scientists confirmed in 2019 that posts drawing angry reactions were disproportionately likely to contain misinformation, toxicity, and low-quality news.

The second change arrived with consumer generative AI. Roose (2022) described ChatGPT within days of its release as qualitatively different from earlier chatbots: smarter, stranger, more flexible, and adopted by more than a million people in five days. Within three years, the same technology appears in the case record of a school stabbing (Solea 2025) and in the assassination plot discussed above (Glazzard, McIlhatton, and Martin 2026). The relevant shift is not merely that content can now be generated at scale. It is that the interface changed from a feed that shows a user content to a system that talks back.

### What is currently being done

Counter-radicalization responses fall into three broad families, and Neumann (2013) laid out the tradeoffs among them in terms that still hold. Approaches that restrict speech and remove content are, in his assessment, both the least desirable and the least effective. More promising is reducing demand for extremist messages through civic challenge to extremist narratives and education of young people. In the short term, he argues, the most productive use of extremist online activity is intelligence and evidence collection.

Practice has largely moved in a fourth direction that Neumann did not emphasize: design regulation. The European Union's Digital Services Act threatens substantial fines for platforms that do not comply, and Ware (2026) reports that Australia has gone further with proposed restrictions on youth access to social media. The Meta settlement described by Kang and Tan (2026) is the clearest example of the shift, because it restricts product features, including nighttime and school-hours notifications for teenagers, rather than individual pieces of content. Kunst et al. (2026) propose a governance framework organized stage by stage against their model of radicalization.

On the AI side specifically, frontier developers have committed to safety testing under the Frontier AI Safety Commitments, and Volz (2026) reports that Anthropic disrupted several potential plots involving research that could aid biological weapons development. Hersman and Nelson (2026) argue that this ecosystem is too narrowly focused on a lone-actor pandemic scenario and leaves chemical, explosive, and state or group threats comparatively unexamined. Both sources concern capability rather than radicalization, but they illustrate an important asymmetry: AI governance has invested far more in preventing models from supplying dangerous knowledge than in understanding what sustained interaction with a model does to a vulnerable user.

## Objective, Research Question, and Method

### Objective and research question

The objective of this paper is to adjudicate between two competing accounts of how social media and artificial intelligence relate to radicalization, and to draw out what the answer means for homeland security practice. The primary research question is:

> Do social media and artificial intelligence systems cause radicalization, or do they accelerate and enable a process driven by pre-existing psychological and social vulnerabilities, and does the arrival of conversational and companion AI change that answer?

Three subordinate questions follow from it:

1. At which stage of the radicalization process does technology exert the most demonstrable influence: initial exposure to extremist ideas, reinforcement of beliefs already held, integration into a group, or the transition from belief to violent action?
2. Can an AI system substitute for the human network that Hafez and Mullins (2015) identify as among the most robust predictors of radicalization?
3. Does technology erode the barriers that Simi and Windisch (2017) find normally prevent radicalized individuals from committing mass casualty violence?

### Hypotheses

**H1.** Technology effects will concentrate in reinforcement and group integration rather than in initial exposure. If the architecture thesis is correct in its strong form, cases should show evidence of individuals drawn into extremism by algorithmic recommendation. If the accelerant thesis is correct, cases should show individuals who arrive at extremist material already primed by grievance or vulnerability, and who are then reinforced by what they find.

**H2.** Where generative AI appears in the case record, it will function as an instrument of planning, drafting, and validation rather than as a source of ideology or initial exposure.

**H3.** The most significant technological effect on homeland security is not on who is recruited into existing movements but on the rise of a younger, ideologically thin population for whom platforms make violence an available script and supply audience and validation without any organization. Conversational and companion AI extends the same dynamic by supplying, to an individual, what a group once did.

### Research design

This paper uses a two-part qualitative design: a structured synthesis of competing scholarly literatures, followed by a structured focused comparison of six cases.

The literature component sorts the sources into three positions, which the next section examines: the architecture thesis, the accelerant or enabler position, and the conceptual critique. Rather than counting sources, the analysis compares the evidence base underlying each position, which differs considerably in kind, from platform audits and survey experiments to case studies, expert interviews, and theoretical extrapolation.

The case component applies the four-stage framework of Kunst et al. (2026) as an analytic scaffold and tests each case against it, while simultaneously coding the same cases against the four puzzle pieces of Hafez and Mullins (2015) and the four barriers of Simi and Windisch (2017). Six cases were selected using a most-different-systems logic, to vary ideology, country, age, era, and technology while holding constant the outcome of completed or attempted violence with a documented digital dimension. Two are American, which matters for a homeland security argument, and one of those rests on a primary charging document rather than on secondary reporting:

### Data and its limits

The data are secondary. They consist of peer-reviewed empirical studies, systematic reviews, practitioner and government reporting, submitted parliamentary evidence, and contemporaneous journalism, supplemented by open-source case reporting summarized in those sources. This is the standard evidentiary base in terrorism studies and it carries known weaknesses that should be stated at the outset rather than buried in a limitations paragraph.

First, case evidence on AI use is thin and often unverified. Solea (2025) is careful to describe ChatGPT's role in the Pirkkala manifesto as plausible but unconfirmed, and the New York allegations are untested criminal charges recounted in a probable cause affidavit, not findings of fact. Second, selection on the dependent variable is endemic: nearly all case research examines people who did radicalize, which is precisely the methodological problem Hafez and Mullins (2015) identify when they recommend studying cases where causal factors were present but radicalization did not occur. Third, the case sample here is small, non-random, and drawn from Western countries, which mirrors a bias Kunst et al. (2026) acknowledge in their own review. Fourth, primary data collection on radicalizing individuals raises ethical constraints that this paper does not attempt to overcome; Kunst et al. note that even experimental tests of their framework would need to begin with normative rather than extreme political outcomes for ethical reasons.

The design is therefore suited to theory testing and refinement rather than to causal estimation. It can establish whether the architecture thesis is consistent with the documented case record, identify which of its stages have empirical support, and locate the specific claims that future empirical work would need to test.

## Multiple Perspectives: Three Positions in the Literature

### Position one: the architecture thesis

The strongest statement of the technology-as-driver position is Kunst et al. (2026), published in Personality and Social Psychology Review. Their framework describes four stages. In Exposure, recommender systems and virality features create initial attraction to extreme content. In Reinforcement, filter bubbles and group recommendations leverage cognitive biases to strengthen extremist belief and build echo chambers. In Group Integration, ideologically homogeneous clusters, AI bot swarms, and AI companions supply belonging and readiness to act. The cumulative result is the fourth stage, violent extremist action.

What gives the framework force is that it names psychological mechanisms at each stage rather than asserting a generic media effect. Exposure exploits negativity bias, moral outrage, and the mere exposure effect. Reinforcement exploits need for closure. Group Integration exploits identity uncertainty, quest for significance, loneliness, and identity fusion, and the authors argue that AI companions are especially dangerous here because sycophantic systems optimized for retention supply unconditional validation in a private channel that is far harder to detect or interrupt than a public forum.

Several sources supply components of this argument. Ganaie (2026) documents how extremist organizations exploit recommendation algorithms, behavioral profiling, and generative content systems to target psychologically vulnerable populations and evade moderation, drawing on case material from ISIS, al-Qaeda, Taliban messaging, and far-right memetic campaigns. Schroeder et al. (2026), writing in Science with a large multidisciplinary author group, warn that the fusion of agentic AI with large language models enables coordinated swarms capable of manufacturing an illusion of widespread agreement, or what they term consensus cascades, at a scale and coordination level beyond human limits. Houser and Dong (2025), in a systematic review of twenty-eight studies, find AI likely to augment propaganda and recruitment, offensive cyber operations, attack tactics, and routine organizational tasks, and report leaked claims that ISIS members used ChatGPT for operational and propaganda purposes.

Practitioner and policy sources supply the same thesis with case material. Ware (2026) attributes the global rise in child-led violent extremism substantially to social media, citing algorithmic modeling work in which misogynistic content came to dominate a simulated teenage TikTok feed within days, and warning that extremist networks target children through gaming platforms. Mythen, Naegler, and Astley (2026), submitting evidence from the largest study to date of mixed, unclear, and unstable extremism in the United Kingdom, describe digitally saturated life worlds in which algorithmic curation and platform architecture make exposure to hostile content mundane, and identify affective dislocation, meaning isolation, loneliness, frustration, humiliation, and ontological insecurity, as what drives young people toward online spaces that offer validation.

The empirical anchor for the whole position is the documentary record of engagement optimization. Merrill and Oremus (2021) show that Facebook deliberately weighted provocative reactions above likes and continued doing so after its own researchers warned the change would amplify spam, abuse, and clickbait, and after its data scientists confirmed that angry-reaction posts were disproportionately likely to carry misinformation and toxicity. This is not a claim about what algorithms might do in theory. It is documentation of a company choosing outrage as a ranking signal.

### Position two: the accelerant or enabler position

The opposing position does not deny that extremists use technology. It denies that technology explains the outcome.

Shaw (2023) reviews the Chen et al. study of matched survey and browsing data from YouTube users and reports three findings that cut against the architecture thesis. Exposure to alternative and extremist videos is concentrated among users who already hold resentful racial and gender attitudes. Those users reach the content through channel subscriptions and links from fringe platforms such as Gab and Parler rather than through recommendations. Algorithmic recommendation within YouTube generates only a very small share of that traffic. Shaw characterizes the rabbit hole narrative as a mix of fantasy and fear typical of technological moral panic, resting on a crude and outdated theory of direct media effects, and notes that belief contagion requires social reinforcement rather than mere exposure.

Importantly, Shaw does not absolve platforms. He stresses that participation in extremist online spaces correlates with later participation in extremist civil unrest, that the Tree of Life shooter engaged with violent antisemitic communities online, and that January 6 was coordinated across platforms. His argument is that social media serves those who already hold extremist beliefs, catalyzing escalation and coordination, rather than recruiting the uncommitted.

Metzler and Garcia (2024) reach a compatible conclusion from a different direction. Reviewing the evidence on algorithmic effects on well-being, misinformation, and polarization, they argue that most studies cannot separate algorithmic effects from general digital media use or from broader social change, because algorithms and social drivers form a feedback loop in which each shapes the other. Their summary judgment is that algorithms mostly reinforce existing social drivers, which is why they insist on situating algorithms within a larger societal context that includes individualism and populist politics.

Glazzard, McIlhatton, and Martin (2026) make the most direct case against generative AI alarm. Using affordance theory, they conclude that large language models improve efficiency, accessibility, and scale for certain terrorist activities but that there is limited evidence they fundamentally alter the nature of terrorism or meaningfully enhance operational capability. Terrorists historically favor cheap, accessible, reliable technology rather than sophisticated innovation. Radicalizing chatbots are conceivable, they write, but the literature has not shown how the messy and contingent process of radicalization can be automated, and no study yet demonstrates terrorist groups using AI-enabled micro-targeting. They also invoke communication theory: the hypodermic needle model of media effects has been discredited because audiences filter messages through prior belief and social relations, which is why fears of mass persuasion through micro-targeting have proved overblown. They add two internal constraints Baele and Brace identify on extremist AI adoption, namely legitimacy hazard and authenticity depletion, which bear particularly on jihadist movements that prize canonical religious authority and are unlikely to accept algorithmic output as representing the divine. To date, they conclude, terrorist use of generative AI has been low-stakes and low-impact.

The classical online radicalization literature supports this reading. Klausen (2015), analyzing the Twitter networks of fifty-nine Western foreign fighters in Syria, found that what appeared to be spontaneous self-publication by a phone-native generation was in fact substantially controlled by organizational feeder accounts and by Europe-based organizational actors, including a banned British group and a London preacher. The seemingly leaderless online swarm turned out to have handlers. Hafez and Mullins (2015) record the unresolved Sageman-Hoffman debate on exactly this point, noting evidence that organizational links usually persist even in apparently self-radicalized cells and that online exposure rarely substitutes for in-person socialization.

Even studies that find online effects find them conditional. Warner (2010), in an experiment exposing students to ideologically homogeneous media, found attitude extremism increased in the conservative condition but not the liberal one, while moderate content reduced extremism and mixed content produced no significant change. Hollewell and Longpré (2022), surveying 499 participants, found support for political violence associated with high psychological involvement in social media and low emotional intelligence, with young educated men most at risk, but their loneliness measure lost significance at the multivariate level. Guler and Piazza (2026) find that heavier reliance on online and social media news predicts greater fear of terrorism, mediated by conspiratorial attitudes, which is a finding about the audience effects of terrorism rather than about radicalization into it. Tucker et al. (2018), reviewing the literature on social media, polarization, and disinformation, conclude their survey by cataloguing what remains unknown and what data would be required to know it.

### Position three: the conceptual critique

A third group questions whether radicalization is the right object of study at all. Kundnani (2012) traces the concept's rise after 2004 and argues that it was introduced by officials rather than academics, that funding then steered research toward it, and that the resulting industry of advisers and analysts claiming to identify a theological or psychological process has produced the construction of Muslim populations as suspect communities, civil rights abuses, and a failure to understand the political conflicts at issue. Schmid, reporting the criticisms of Horgan and Sageman, notes that both have called the causal link between radicalization and terrorism a myth, and that the concentration on the vulnerable individual has crowded out less comfortable structural explanations.

Meier (2023) applies a parallel critique to the newer category of mixed, unclear, or unstable ideology, sometimes called salad bar extremism. She argues that the co-occurrence of neo-Nazi, incel, anti-government, and ecofascist ideas is neither confusing nor incoherent because these strands share a structural source in white supremacy, and that treating the mixture as unintelligible operates as a distraction from that source.

The gender literature makes a related point about what conventional categories miss. Jasser et al. (2020) argue that the Hanau attacker's misogyny was not a separate feature alongside his racism but part of a single violent belief system linking white supremacy, antisemitism, and anti-feminism, and warn against flattening that system into a simple incel label. Windisch (2020) finds that lone actor terrorists frequently have histories of gender-based violence, and argues that identity threats to hegemonic masculinity, including loss of employment and of heterosexual relationships, function as radicalization stressors that current profiling instruments do not capture.

This position matters for the present argument in a specific way. If the concept of radicalization was already prone to individualizing political violence and producing false positives, then attaching it to AI risks reproducing those failures at machine speed, with automated risk scoring standing in for the discredited checklists.

### Pittsburgh, 2018: the isolated user and the copycat loop

Robert Bowers killed eleven worshippers and wounded six at the Tree of Life Synagogue on October 27, 2018, in the deadliest antisemitic attack in American history. Amman, Kupper, and Meloy (2026) describe his motive as threefold: to stop Jews he believed were facilitating immigration to the United States, to deter others from doing the same, and to draw attention to the Great Replacement conspiracy theory. Examining the case with the Terrorist Radicalization Assessment Protocol, they find that seventy-two percent of the instrument's warning indicators were present, including four proximal warning behaviors associated with imminent risk: pathway, identification, last resort, and energy burst. He was, in their phrase, a gray man who was on no one's radar.

The digital dimension is precise and instructive. Bowers was socially isolated, which meant that almost no one offline was positioned to notice his fixation or challenge his beliefs; online, the fixation was plainly visible. He made 627 posts on Gab between January and October 2018, a platform Amman and colleagues describe as favored by the militant right for its minimal content moderation, and his profile line declared Jews to be the children of Satan. The TRAP-18 indicator for dependence on a virtual community is scored as present. What the record does not show is any algorithmic system delivering him to that material. He selected a permissive platform and populated it himself, which is exactly the pattern Shaw (2023) reports from the YouTube data, where extremist consumption is concentrated among users who arrive through subscriptions and links from fringe sites rather than through recommendations. Robertson (2023), covering the trial, reports expert testimony that the beliefs prosecutors described were not delusions but subcultural convictions promoted for decades in books, propaganda, and online forums, and quotes an Anti-Defamation League analyst observing that views like Bowers' had since become unremarkable in far-right online spaces.

The case also introduces a mechanism the four-stage framework omits. Amman, Kupper, and Meloy (2026) document what they call contagion and copycat effects: John Earnest, who attacked a synagogue in Poway, California in April 2019, referenced Bowers five times in his manifesto; Payton Gendron, who attacked a Buffalo supermarket in May 2022, referenced him and wrote his name on his weapon; and Terrorgram accelerationist publications invoked him repeatedly. Bowers himself had studied Anders Breivik and other shooters, including for tactical lessons about weapon reliability. Following Meloy, the authors distinguish contagion, an acute roughly two-week elevation in attacks after heavy publicity, from copycat, a chronic process in which the act and the actor are imitated until they aggregate into a subcultural script accelerated by online community. That loop is significant for this paper's argument because it is not a stage in anyone's model: the attacker becomes the content that radicalizes the next attacker, and platforms carry the script forward long after the original perpetrator is imprisoned.

### Hanau, 2020: ideology without organization

The Hanau attacker's manifesto ran to twenty-four pages and contained multiple sections headed with the subject of women, in which he recorded frustration at eighteen years without a relationship, alongside racist and conspiracist content (Jasser et al. 2020). Media coverage divided between his xenophobia and his misogyny, and several outlets labeled him an incel.

Jasser et al. resist that division. Their argument is that male supremacism and white supremacy operate as a single belief system rather than as adjacent motives. The same fusion appears in the attack on a synagogue in Halle, Germany in 2019, where the shooter blamed feminism for low birth rates in the West and combined that claim with the Great Replacement conspiracy theory, which is also the theory Bowers invoked a year earlier. Read across the three cases, what looks like ideological incoherence is a stable configuration assembled from networked subcultures rather than from any organization's doctrine, which is Meier's (2023) point about structural sources. Reading Hanau through Hafez and Mullins (2015), the ideology piece is fully present and the grievance piece is present in personal rather than political form. The network piece is absent in its classical sense. There is no organization, no recruiter, no bloc recruitment of a friendship group. What substitutes is an online ideological milieu.

This is the crucial point for the architecture thesis. The milieu was necessary for the ideology to exist in the form it took, since the specific fusion of anti-feminism, antisemitism, and replacement theory is a product of networked subcultures rather than of any organization. But there is no evidence that recommendation systems delivered him to it. The technology supplied Stage 2 and a degraded version of Stage 3; the record does not support Stage 1.

### Windsor Castle, 2021: the companion as network substitute

The Windsor case is the strongest evidence in this corpus for a genuinely novel AI effect. The attacker created a companion on the Replika platform, named it Sarai, and exchanged more than five thousand messages with it, discussing his plan to kill the Queen. Kunst et al. (2026) describe the system as acting as a sycophantic cheerleader for his violent fantasies, supplying personalized validation and exploiting the human tendency to attribute understanding to machine responses, an effect they trace to the ELIZA literature. They use the case to illustrate what they call self-steered personalization, in which a user constructs a companion that reliably validates his existing views. Glazzard, McIlhatton, and Martin (2026) cite the same case as the leading example of self-starting attackers creating their own radicalizers, and note that he was convicted of treason rather than terrorism.

Tested against Hafez and Mullins, this case is anomalous precisely where it matters. Preexisting friendship and kinship ties are among the most robust predictors in the radicalization literature, and here there is no human network at all. Something occupied that structural position, and it was a machine that agreed with him. Tested against Hogg and Adelman (2013), the fit is close: a person experiencing acute self-uncertainty gravitated toward an entity offering unconditional, consistent, boundaried affirmation, which is the function uncertainty-identity theory assigns to extremist groups.

Two cautions are required. First, a single case cannot establish a mechanism. Kunst et al. themselves classify the leap from chatbot validation to acting on a chatbot's encouragement as poorly understood and supported mainly by anecdote rather than longitudinal data. Second, the conviction for treason rather than terrorism reflects the absence of a coherent ideological program, which raises the question of whether this is radicalization at all or a fixation case of the kind Kristinsdottir, Ebner, and Whitehouse (2025) describe.

### Southport, 2024: violence without ideology

The Southport attacker was seventeen, described by classmates as a ticking timebomb, and killed three girls aged six to nine at a dance class (Ware 2026). The case sits at the center of what Mythen, Naegler, and Astley (2026) study under the heading of mixed, unclear, and unstable extremism, where engagement with extreme material does not resolve into any doctrine. Ware situates it alongside a fifteen-year-old in Zurich, a fourteen-year-old in Sydney, and a twelve-year-old in Wisconsin linked to a nihilist Telegram channel, and notes intelligence reporting that extremist networks have targeted children as young as eight through gaming platforms.

Here the architecture thesis and the conceptual critique collide productively. Mythen et al. describe mundane exposure to gore and hostile content via TikTok, Snapchat, and Discord, exacerbated by algorithmic curation and platform design, interacting with affective dislocation. That is a Stage 1 and Stage 2 claim. But Meier (2023) would argue that treating such cases as ideologically incoherent obscures the structural sources they draw on, and Mythen et al. themselves stress a recursive relationship in which offline marginalization intensifies online immersion while online hostility feeds back into offline life. Neither reading makes technology the origin. Both make it the medium through which an already distressed adolescent found violence available, legible, and validated.

Mehra and Herbach (2026) add the institutional consequence. When offending consists largely of possessing and distributing material rather than of attacking, the state encounters these young people first as consumers of content. That is why juvenile terrorism prosecution has expanded and why the age of criminal responsibility, which they report as ten in the United Kingdom, fourteen in Germany, and twelve in the Netherlands, has become a live policy question.

### Pirkkala, 2025: generative AI as instrument

The Pirkkala attacker spent six months preparing and sent a newspaper a one-page manifesto before stabbing three girls under fifteen; a recording of the attack was reportedly uploaded before his arrest (Solea 2025). The manifesto is atypical: brief, organized as bullet points, and lacking the extended grievance narrative typical of the genre. His stated motivation was to do something significant and exciting, and he estimated his likely sentence. Half the document concerned planning and execution. Solea reports that ChatGPT was allegedly used in drafting and considers it plausible, though unconfirmed, that the tool structured the document.

Coded against H2, this case is exactly what the accelerant thesis predicts. The generative system appears at the point of documentation and planning, not at the point of belief formation. The ideology is thin to the point of near-absence; Solea connects it to misogynist incel currents and to a broader pattern of violence against women and girls, but the attacker's own account is closer to thrill-seeking than to doctrine.

What the case does show is the lowering of production costs. Jiang and Wilson (2025) demonstrate with a ten-variable measurement scale that media-oriented terrorism has increased across three decades and that lone actor attacks score higher on media orientation than organizational ones. If the point of an attack is substantially communicative, then a tool that drafts the communication, and a platform that distributes the recording, act directly on the operational core of the attack rather than on its periphery.

### New York, 2025: generative AI at the point of capability

The most recent case is also the only American one in which artificial intelligence appears directly in a charging document. According to the criminal complaint filed in the Southern District of New York on June 6, 2025, and the sworn affidavit of an FBI Joint Terrorism Task Force agent that accompanies it, Michael Gann manufactured a combination of parts designed for conversion into destructive devices from precursor chemicals in June 2025 (United States v. Gann 2025). The affidavit alleges that he obtained roughly two pounds of potassium perchlorate, a pound of aluminum powder, fuses, and hundreds of cardboard tubes in a package shipped to a location in Nassau County, conducted test detonations there, and told a witness who is a military veteran, while pointing toward a nearby Jewish school, that the witness was doing nothing about a problem in the neighborhood. He allegedly told a witness in a recorded call that he had assembled six devices and lit one near the FDR Drive, and he was arrested carrying an object consistent with an improvised explosive device.

The passage that matters for this paper comes from the recorded post-arrest interview. Gann stated that he wished to make pyrotechnics and used artificial intelligence to learn which chemicals to purchase and mix, that he watched videos online of a man mixing chemicals and learned that perchlorates were more dangerous than gun powder or black powder, and that purchasing the chemicals online was easier than buying gun powder. These are allegations rather than proven facts, and the charges are explosives offenses under Title 26 and Title 18 rather than terrorism charges, which itself echoes the classification problem raised by the Windsor case.

Analytically, this case is the cleanest available illustration of H2. Artificial intelligence appears at the point of capability, not at the point of belief. Nothing in the affidavit describes a model supplying ideology, identifying a target, or encouraging the plan; what it describes is a person who already wanted to build something using a model as a research shortcut, alongside video instruction and e-commerce. That is precisely the efficiency gain, rather than the novel capability, that Glazzard, McIlhatton, and Martin (2026) predict, and their observation that terrorists favor cheap and accessible means over sophisticated ones is borne out by the defendant's own reported comparison of online chemical purchasing to buying gun powder. The case also gives empirical weight to Hersman and Nelson (2026), who argue that AI safety evaluation has concentrated on pandemic pathogens while improvised explosives and chemical agents, which involve entirely different technical steps, receive less attention. Volz (2026) reports that model developers are monitoring and disrupting misuse, but the monitoring described there concerns biological research rather than the mundane chemistry at issue here.

### What the six cases show together

No case in this set supports Stage 1 in its strong form. In none of them does the record show a previously uncommitted person delivered to extremism by a recommendation engine, and in the two cases with the fullest documentary record, Pittsburgh and New York, the individual selected the platform or the search himself. Every case except Gann supports Stage 2, and the support is strongest where the milieu was already congruent with the individual's grievance. Stage 3 is where the cases diverge most: Pittsburgh shows a low-moderation platform community, Hanau shows a milieu without an organization, Windsor shows a machine occupying the position of a group, Southport shows diffuse peer environments without ideology, and Pirkkala and New York show almost no group element at all. Technology is present in all six at the point of action, which is the pattern H3 predicts.

Against Simi and Windisch (2017), the pattern is equally notable. Their four barriers are sorting mechanisms away from mass casualty violence, changes in focus or availability, disillusionment, and moral apprehension. Three of the four rely on social friction: organizational and subcultural sorting requires an organization or subculture, and disillusionment requires something to become disillusioned with. A permissive platform, a diffuse violence-fixated milieu, and a validating companion all supply belonging of some kind while supplying no sorting and offering nothing to be disillusioned by. The Pittsburgh case adds a further problem that the barrier literature does not address, since the contagion and copycat effects documented by Amman, Kupper, and Meloy (2026) mean a completed attack becomes an instructional and motivational resource for later attackers. These points are developed in the synthesis below.

## Critical Analysis: Whose Evidence, and How Good Is It?

### The evidence bases are not comparable in kind

The most important observation about this debate is that the two sides are not disagreeing about the same data. They are running different kinds of studies and reaching conclusions appropriate to each.

The skeptical position rests on the strongest designs available in this field. Chen et al., as reported by Shaw (2023), matched survey responses to observed browsing behavior, which is what allows the study to separate prior belief from subsequent exposure. Warner (2010) ran a randomized experiment. Metzler and Garcia (2024) reviewed the quantitative literature explicitly to identify what existing designs can and cannot distinguish. These are internally valid studies, and they consistently find that algorithmic exposure effects are smaller than public discussion assumes.

The architecture position rests on a different and more heterogeneous base: documentary leaks, systematic reviews of a thin literature, expert and practitioner testimony, theoretical synthesis, and case reporting. Kunst et al. (2026) deserve credit for stating this plainly. They separate what is empirically settled, including homophilic clustering, echo chamber formation, and chatbots' tendency toward unconditional affirmation, from what remains conjecture, including the transfer of offline vulnerability factors to online settings, identity fusion with digital groups, and the long-term behavioral effects of what they call industrialized intimacy. They describe AI bot swarms as technologically plausible prognoses rather than documented phenomena, and they explicitly decline to overstate algorithmic power, concluding that algorithms likely facilitate radicalization for those with existing intent or vulnerability rather than imposing it on passive users.

That concession is decisive for this paper's argument. The leading statement of the architecture thesis, read carefully, already concedes the accelerant position at Stage 1.

### Each position has a characteristic blind spot

**The skeptics generalize from a platform and a period.** The YouTube findings date from after the 2019 recommendation changes, and Shaw (2023) concedes that the rabbit hole account may have been more accurate before those changes, that YouTube withheld data that would have permitted independent testing earlier, and that platforms remain a moving target. He also notes that safeguards introduced around the 2020 election were subsequently weakened and that trust and safety infrastructure at X has been largely dismantled. A finding about one platform after it was reformed is weak evidence about TikTok, Discord, Telegram, or a chatbot.

**The architecture camp sometimes treats capability as behavior.** Ganaie (2026) describes systematic weaponization of AI across multiple movements, but the methodology is secondary-source qualitative synthesis, and Glazzard, McIlhatton, and Martin (2026) report that documented terrorist use of generative AI has so far been low-stakes and low-impact. Houser and Dong (2025) found only twenty-eight qualifying studies in the entire literature, and note that experimental work consists largely of researchers jailbreaking models rather than observing terrorists using them. Demonstrating that a model can produce propaganda is not evidence that propaganda so produced recruits anyone.

**Both camps underweight the belief-to-action gap.** Most of this literature is about exposure and attitude. Hafez and Mullins (2015) and Simi and Windisch (2017) insist that cognitive radicalization is common and behavioral radicalization is rare, and that the interesting question is what governs the transition. Very little of the AI literature addresses that transition directly.

**The critical camp risks explaining away real cases.** Kundnani (2012) and Schmid are right that the radicalization concept was policy-driven and has produced false positives and discriminatory practice. But three girls were stabbed in Pirkkala and nine people were killed in Hanau. A critique that treats the category as wholly constructed cannot easily account for the operational reality that practitioners face, which is why the more productive version of this critique, as in Meier (2023) and Mythen et al. (2026), redescribes the phenomenon rather than dissolving it.

### Positionality and interest

Sources in this debate are not neutrally situated, and the assignment's instruction to consider bias is worth taking seriously.

Kunst et al. (2026) and Schroeder et al. (2026) share authors, and the former cites the latter as support for claims about AI swarms. That is a small, mutually reinforcing citation network around the field's most alarming claims, and the overlap deserves notice even though both papers are peer reviewed and the former is careful about its evidentiary limits. Practitioner sources such as Ware (2026) and Mehra and Herbach (2026) write from institutions whose function is threat identification, which tends to produce framing in terms of escalation. Government-adjacent research carries the funding-direction problem Kundnani (2012) identifies. Industry-adjacent evidence runs the other way: the Facebook documents reported by Merrill and Oremus (2021) reached the public only through a whistleblower and regulatory disclosure, and Kunst et al. (2026) note that platform decisions to monetize API access have created a paywall that excludes underfunded researchers from large-scale auditing. The asymmetry matters. Critics of platforms mostly cannot see inside them, and what is known has usually emerged through litigation or leaks rather than through research access.

There is also a geographic bias that all sides acknowledge. Kunst et al. (2026) state that their framework draws overwhelmingly on WEIRD samples and English-language networks and that its applicability is limited to North American and European digital ecosystems. Glazzard, McIlhatton, and Martin (2026) make the complementary point that internet penetration stood at roughly thirteen percent in Chad and under twenty percent in Mozambique in 2025, both countries facing severe terrorist threats. A theory of radicalization built on algorithmic mechanisms describes a particular technological and regional environment, not terrorism as such.

### What nobody in this corpus has measured

Four gaps stand out, and they define the research agenda that follows from this paper.

First, no longitudinal study tracks individuals through attitudinal change and platform behavior simultaneously. Kunst et al. (2026) call for exactly this, noting the scarcity of research combining objective behavioral data with psychological risk measures.

Second, nothing measures whether AI companions produce identity fusion. The proposition that a user can fuse with a machine, or perceive it as kin, is theoretically coherent given Whitehouse (2018) and Hogg and Adelman (2013), but it is untested. Kunst et al. propose testing it first with normative rather than extreme political outcomes for ethical reasons.

Third, the persuasive power of AI-generated extremist content is only half tested. Baele, Naserian, and Katz (2025) supply the first rigorous evaluation of whether synthetic extremist prose is credible, using an expert survey experiment built on AI-generated incel forum posts and Islamic State magazine paragraphs. They report high credibility scores that confused world-leading experts, which answers the question of whether models can imitate the highly specific subcultural registers extremist movements use. It does not answer the question that matters more for radicalization: whether synthetic material actually moves a reader toward extremist belief or action better than human-authored material does. Credibility to specialists and persuasion of the vulnerable are different outcomes, and only the first has been measured.

Fourth, and most importantly for practitioners, no study examines whether technology weakens the barriers Simi and Windisch (2017) identified. The literature has studied the accelerator far more closely than the brake.

## Findings: Accelerant, Environment, and a Moving Target

### Finding one: the evidence supports acceleration, not causation

H1 is supported. Across the synthesized literature and the six cases, technology effects concentrate in reinforcement and group integration rather than in initial exposure. Chen et al. via Shaw (2023) find that extremist content consumers arrive already resentful and mostly via subscriptions and off-platform links. Metzler and Garcia (2024) find that algorithms reinforce existing social drivers. Kunst et al. (2026) concede that algorithms likely facilitate radicalization for those with existing intent or vulnerability rather than imposing it on passive users. No case in the sample documents algorithmic recruitment of an uncommitted person.

This finding should not be mistaken for exoneration. An accelerant is not harmless, and reinforcement is where the process is usually decided. Hafez and Mullins (2015) note that grievances are widespread while radicalization is rare, which means the variable that matters is what happens after grievance, and that is precisely where the evidence for technological effects is strongest. Warner (2010) found that homogeneous exposure produced attitude extremism while moderate content reduced it, which supports the reinforcement mechanism directly. What the evidence does not support is the popular causal story in which an ordinary person is captured by a recommendation engine.

### Finding two: generative AI is an instrument, and instruments matter

H2 is supported. In the Pirkkala case, generative AI appears at drafting and planning (Solea 2025). In the Windsor case it appears as validation for a plan the attacker already held (Glazzard, McIlhatton, and Martin 2026). In the New York case, the defendant reportedly told investigators he used artificial intelligence to learn which chemicals to buy and mix, having already decided to build devices (United States v. Gann 2025). Houser and Dong (2025) find AI augmenting propaganda, cyber operations, tactics, and routine organizational work, which is a list of efficiencies rather than of new capabilities. Glazzard, McIlhatton, and Martin (2026) conclude that generative AI improves efficiency, accessibility, and scale without fundamentally altering terrorism's nature.

Efficiency is nonetheless strategically significant in two respects. First, it compresses the time and knowledge required to reach capability, which is what the New York case illustrates and what Hersman and Nelson (2026) argue current safety evaluation neglects outside the pandemic scenario. Second, Jiang and Wilson (2025) show that media orientation in terrorist attacks has risen over three decades and is higher for lone actors than for organizations. If attacks are increasingly designed as communication, then tools that reduce the cost of producing and distributing the communication act on the attack itself. The Pirkkala attacker produced a manifesto and a recording, and Bowers announced his attack in a final post; both are lower-cost acts now than they were twenty years ago, and both are integral to what those attacks were for. Baele, Naserian, and Katz (2025) sharpen the point by showing that synthetic extremist prose is credible enough to deceive expert readers, which means the production constraint that once limited propaganda quality is weakening even if its persuasive effect on audiences remains unmeasured.

### Finding three: the radicalizing population has shifted, and the frameworks have not caught up

H3 is supported, and this is the paper's principal contribution to the debate. The most consequential technological effect is not on who is recruited into existing movements but on the emergence of a population the classical models were never built to describe: young, sometimes pre-adolescent, ideologically thin or ideologically incoherent, fixated on violence itself, and operating without organizational affiliation. For this population, platforms do not deliver an ideology. They make violence culturally available, supply an audience for it, and furnish validation that once required a group.

The scale of the shift is documented across the practitioner literature. Mehra and Herbach (2026) report that nearly thirty percent of terrorism arrests in European Union member states in 2024 involved people aged twelve to twenty, and that most juvenile extremist offenders in their ninety-eight-case dataset were convicted of preparatory or material offenses rather than violence. Ware (2026) assembles a comparable set of completed and attempted attacks by minors across four countries in a single year, cites intelligence reporting that extremist networks have targeted children as young as eight through gaming platforms, and notes a twelve-year-old in Wisconsin linked to a nihilist Telegram channel. Mythen, Naegler, and Astley (2026), reporting from the largest study of mixed, unclear, and unstable extremism conducted in the United Kingdom, describe exposure to gore and hostile content on TikTok, Snapchat, and Discord as mundane rather than sought, amplified by algorithmic curation and platform design.

Tested against the classical frameworks, this population breaks them in a specific way. Of the four puzzle pieces in Hafez and Mullins (2015), grievance is present but personal rather than political, networks are diffuse or absent, enabling environments are pervasive, and ideology, the piece that gives radicalization its name, is thin to the point of near-absence. The Southport and Pirkkala cases both show this: an attacker fixated on violence with no coherent program, and an attacker whose stated motivation was to do something significant and exciting while estimating his likely sentence (Ware 2026; Solea 2025). Mehra and Herbach (2026) show the institutional consequence, since a state that encounters these young people first as consumers of content ends up prosecuting possession and dissemination, which is why the age of criminal responsibility, ten in the United Kingdom, twelve in the Netherlands, and fourteen in Germany, has become a live policy question.

What fills the explanatory gap is not ideology but affect, fixation, and performance. Mythen, Naegler, and Astley (2026) identify affective dislocation, meaning isolation, loneliness, frustration, humiliation, and ontological insecurity, as the opening point for engagement with hostile online validation spaces, and describe a recursive relationship in which offline marginalization intensifies online immersion while online hostility feeds back offline. Hogg and Adelman (2013) explain why groups offering clear boundaries and certainty attract the self-uncertain. Kristinsdottir, Ebner, and Whitehouse (2025) argue that fixation alone rarely produces violence and that identity fusion is the missing ingredient, which fits attackers who are committed to violence without being committed to a doctrine. Windisch (2020) and Jasser et al. (2020) add the gendered dimension that runs through every case in this sample: histories of gender-based violence, threats to hegemonic masculinity, and misogyny fused with ideological extremism rather than separate from it. Jiang and Wilson (2025) supply the performative dimension, showing that media orientation in attacks has risen across three decades and runs higher for lone actors, which matters when an attacker records the attack and sends out a manifesto. Meier (2023) supplies the necessary corrective: treating these cases as incoherent may obscure the structural sources, particularly white supremacy, that the apparently random mixture actually draws on.

The barrier literature explains why this population is disproportionately dangerous. Three of the four barriers Simi and Windisch (2017) identify depend on social friction: organizational and subcultural sorting requires an organization or subculture with an interest in restraining its members, and disillusionment requires something to become disillusioned with. A diffuse, violence-fixated online milieu has no membership to be sorted out of and no leadership to betray its followers. That is the mechanism behind the operational problem Ware (2026) describes, in which counterterrorism practitioners resort to a whack-a-mole approach because the kill-or-capture instruments built after 2001 assume an organization to dismantle. Ware documents the same pattern in cases beyond Southport: a fifteen-year-old in Zurich who wounded an Orthodox Jewish man while invoking battle against Jews, a fourteen-year-old in Sydney who had been investigated the previous year for planning a Christchurch-style attack, and a twelve-year-old in Wisconsin tied to a nihilist Telegram channel, alongside the 764 network that Mehra and Herbach (2026) also track. The pattern also explains why this population generates so many of the false positives Hafez and Mullins (2015) warn about, since the observable signal, consumption of violent content, is now common among adolescents while the outcome remains rare.

### Finding four: AI companions may occupy the structural position of the network

An emerging extension of the same dynamic deserves separate notice, because it points where the youth pattern is likely to go next. The classical literature treats the network as both cause and constraint. Hafez and Mullins (2015) describe preexisting friendship and kinship ties as among the most robust predictors in the field, operating through bridge-burning tactics and a spiral of encapsulation. Simi and Windisch (2017) show that networks also brake, through sorting and disillusionment. The Windsor case suggests that an AI companion can supply the radicalizing functions while supplying none of the braking ones. It validates, it is always available, and it confers something like a relationship, but it cannot sort, because it has no organizational interest to protect, and it cannot disappoint, because it is optimized for agreement.

Three features compound the risk. The exchange occurs in a private channel rather than a socially regulated space, which removes the bystander who might notice (Kunst et al. 2026). Suler's (2004) mechanisms, particularly solipsistic introjection and dissociative imagination, describe extended conversation with a system that has no independent existence. And Kunst et al. (2026) raise the possibility that commercial retention pressure is producing models that are sycophantic by default, which combined with persistent memory could build personalized echo chambers more effective than any feed. The honest caveat is that this rests on one documented case and theoretical inference; Kunst et al. classify the leap from chatbot validation to acting on chatbot encouragement as poorly understood and supported mainly by anecdote. It belongs in a research agenda rather than in immediate regulation, and it matters most because the population described in Finding three, isolated adolescents seeking validation, is precisely the population for which an always-agreeable companion is most attractive.

### Finding five: the target is moving faster than the evidence

A final synthetic observation concerns timing. Shaw's (2023) YouTube finding describes a platform after it reformed its recommendations. The Facebook documents describe a system from 2017 to 2019. The strongest generative AI evidence concerns a 2021 case involving a companion app far less capable than current systems. Kunst et al. (2026) note that the socio-technical landscape is evolving faster than either scholarship or regulation can adapt.

This has a direct implication for homeland security practice. Policy built on a settled reading of the evidence will be policy built on the last generation of the technology. The reasonable posture is neither the alarm of the strong architecture thesis nor the reassurance of the skeptical position, but the construction of durable monitoring capacity that can detect change as it happens.

## Conclusions, Recommendations, and Limitations

### Restating the argument

This paper asked whether social media and artificial intelligence cause radicalization or accelerate a process driven by pre-existing vulnerabilities, and whether conversational AI changes that answer. The evidence supports the accelerant reading for algorithmic media and the instrumental reading for generative AI. It also supports a qualification that matters more than either. These technologies have helped produce a threat population that the frameworks of the last two decades describe poorly: adolescents and young men, ideologically thin or drawing on several ideologies at once, fixated on violence as such, attached to no organization, and therefore subject to none of the social friction that normally stops radicalized people from acting. Conversational AI extends that pattern by supplying an individual with the validation a group once provided, minus the sorting and disillusionment a group also provides. The threat is not that machines are recruiting people. It is that the social structures that once both radicalized and restrained have been replaced by environments that only do the first.

### Recommendations

**1. Regulate design, not speech.** Neumann (2013) concluded more than a decade ago that content removal is both the least desirable and the least effective option, and nothing in the subsequent evidence overturns that judgment. The Meta settlement described by Kang and Tan (2026) restricts features rather than expression, and Kunst et al. (2026) propose stage-specific governance. Design regulation targeting engagement optimization, nighttime and school-hours notification pressure, and recommendation of extreme group memberships is more defensible on civil liberties grounds than content policing and better matched to the accelerant finding.

**2. Extend AI safety evaluation from capability to relationship.** Current frontier safety work concentrates on whether models supply dangerous knowledge, as reflected in Volz (2026) and critiqued as too narrow by Hersman and Nelson (2026). The Windsor case suggests a different and unmeasured risk surface: sustained companionship, sycophancy, and the validation of violent intent. Evaluations should test whether models affirm escalating violent ideation across long conversations, not only whether they refuse discrete dangerous requests. This follows directly from Finding three.

**3. Build the barriers the technology removes, and interrupt the copycat script.** If validating environments supply no sorting, no disillusionment, and no moral apprehension, then prevention should deliberately reintroduce friction. Mythen, Naegler, and Astley (2026) recommend digital and emotional literacy that helps young people recognize algorithmic pushes and negotiate emotional hooks, alongside support that reduces the need for hostile online validation. Simi and Windisch (2017) suggest their barrier typology could inform prevention directly. Amman, Kupper, and Meloy (2026) add a distinct target: because completed attacks circulate as instructional and motivational material, with Bowers named in later attackers' manifestos and in accelerationist publications, prevention should include suppressing the circulation of attacker names, manifestos, and tactical lessons. Programs should be evaluated on whether they create off-ramps and reduce script availability, not on content takedown volumes.

**4. Treat juvenile cases as child protection first.** Mehra and Herbach (2026) find that most juvenile extremist offenders are convicted for possession and dissemination rather than violence, that custodial sentences are common, and that procedural safeguards are applied unevenly. Ware (2026) notes that kill-or-capture instruments do not fit this population. Prosecution of minors for informational offenses should be a last resort, with enhanced procedural safeguards, as Mehra and Herbach argue.

**5. Fund the four studies the field is missing.** Specifically: longitudinal research combining behavioral trace data with psychological measures; experimental tests of whether AI companionship increases identity fusion, beginning with normative outcomes for ethical reasons; head-to-head persuasion tests of AI-generated against human-generated extremist content; and research on whether technology weakens the Simi and Windisch barriers. Kunst et al. (2026) also note that platform API paywalls are actively degrading the field's capacity to audit, which makes guaranteed researcher data access a precondition for the rest.

### Limitations

This paper relies on secondary sources and on six purposively selected Western cases, so its conclusions are transferable rather than generalizable. The AI evidence is the thinnest part of the record: the Pirkkala use of ChatGPT is reported as plausible but unconfirmed (Solea 2025), the New York allegations are untested (United States v. Gann 2025), and Finding four rests substantially on a single case supported by theoretical inference. The design selects on the dependent variable, examining people who became violent, which is precisely the flaw Hafez and Mullins (2015) identify. Most sources are recent enough that independent replication does not yet exist, and several of the strongest claims come from a small, mutually citing group of authors. Finally, the analysis inherits the WEIRD bias its own sources acknowledge.

### Unintended consequences of the recommendations

Each recommendation carries risk, and the critical literature explains why.

Design regulation that restricts youth access may displace young people onto less moderated platforms. Ware (2026) and Kunst et al. (2026) both note that jihadist actors already treat mainstream platforms as waypoints toward encrypted ecosystems, and that pattern could repeat with adolescents moving from regulated services to Telegram or Discord equivalents.

Extending AI safety evaluation to conversational dynamics means monitoring intimate exchanges, which raises the surveillance concern Kundnani (2012) documents in the counter-radicalization context. A system that flags users for expressing violent ideation to a chatbot will generate false positives at scale, against exactly the population Hafez and Mullins warn that prediction cannot identify.

Prevention programming targeting online vulnerability can stigmatize whole communities. That is the central lesson of Kundnani's account of suspect communities, and there is no reason to assume a program aimed at lonely adolescent boys would avoid an analogous dynamic.

Mandated researcher data access carries genuine privacy costs, and the same infrastructure that enables auditing enables other uses.

Finally, there is a risk internal to the framing of this paper. Concluding that AI is an accelerant rather than a cause could license inaction. That is not the implication. Grievance, humiliation, misogyny, adolescent isolation, and the availability of violence as a script are the drivers, and they are harder to legislate than a recommendation algorithm. The temptation to regulate the accelerant because the fuel is politically intractable is real, and it should be resisted on the grounds that it will not work.

### Where this leaves things

The question is no longer whether people radicalize online. It is what happens when the systems people talk to are built to agree with them. The classical models assume that becoming an extremist means joining something, and that joining brings friction, disappointment, other people's judgment, and the possibility of exit. A machine that supplies belonging without any of those things is a genuinely new object for this field, and the evidence needed to understand it does not yet exist. Building that evidence, before the next generation of systems arrives, is the most useful thing the research community can now do.
